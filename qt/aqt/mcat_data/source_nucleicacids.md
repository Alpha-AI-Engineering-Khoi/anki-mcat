# Nucleic acids

Nucleic acids are large biomolecules found in every cell and in viruses, and they are the molecules that store and transmit genetic information. Chemically they are linear polymers, meaning long unbranched chains built from repeating monomer units called nucleotides. The two major classes are deoxyribonucleic acid (DNA) and ribonucleic acid (RNA), which differ mainly in their sugar. They were named for their initial discovery inside the cell nucleus and for their phosphate groups, which make them acidic; most living cells contain both DNA and RNA, whereas a virus carries either DNA or RNA but usually not both.

Each nucleotide is made of three parts: a nitrogenous base, a five-carbon (pentose) sugar, and one or more phosphate groups. The sugar is ribose in RNA and deoxyribose in DNA, and these two sugars differ only by a single hydroxyl group. A base joined to a sugar alone is called a nucleoside; when one or more phosphate groups are also attached, the whole unit becomes a nucleotide. A nucleotide with a single phosphate is a nucleoside monophosphate, and adding a second or third phosphate gives a nucleoside diphosphate or a nucleoside triphosphate.

The five standard bases are divided into two chemical families. Purines, namely adenine (A) and guanine (G), are the larger bases, each built from a fused double-ring structure, while pyrimidines, namely cytosine (C), thymine (T), and uracil (U), are smaller single-ring molecules. Adenine, guanine, and cytosine occur in both DNA and RNA; thymine is found in DNA, and in RNA it is replaced by uracil, which differs from thymine only by lacking a methyl group on its ring.

Within a single strand, adjacent nucleotides are linked by covalent phosphodiester bonds, in which a phosphate group bridges the 3' carbon of one sugar and the 5' carbon of the next. This produces a repeating sugar-phosphate backbone from which the bases project. Because the two ends of a strand are chemically different, one carrying a free phosphate on its 5' carbon and the other a free hydroxyl on its 3' carbon, every strand has a defined direction, or polarity, described as running from its 5' end to its 3' end. The enzymes that synthesize DNA and RNA build the new strand only in the 5'-to-3' direction.

In its usual form, DNA is not a single strand but two polynucleotide chains wound around a common axis into a double helix. The helix is held together by hydrogen bonds between the paired bases and is stabilized further by stacking interactions between neighboring bases. The two strands point in opposite directions, one running 5' to 3' and its partner 3' to 5', so they are described as antiparallel, and in the common B-form the helix twists to the right.

The two strands are held together by specific, complementary base pairing, in which each base pairs with only one partner on the opposite strand. Adenine pairs with thymine through two hydrogen bonds, and guanine pairs with cytosine through three hydrogen bonds, so a larger purine is always matched with a smaller pyrimidine and the helix keeps a uniform width. Because the pairing rules are fixed, the sequence of one strand automatically specifies the sequence of the other, so each strand can act as a template to rebuild its partner during replication. Since the hydrogen bonds are noncovalent, the two strands can be pulled apart like a zipper whenever the DNA must be copied or read.

The genetic information itself is stored in the linear order of the four bases along a strand. A segment of DNA that carries the instructions for a functional product is called a gene, and the base sequence of a gene ultimately specifies the amino acid sequence of a protein through the genetic code, which reads the bases in non-overlapping three-letter units called codons. To use this information, a cell first copies a gene's sequence into a complementary RNA molecule in a process called transcription, and that RNA is then decoded to assemble a protein in a process called translation.

A eukaryotic cell holds far more DNA than would fit in its nucleus unaided, so the DNA is packaged together with proteins into a compact complex called chromatin. The central packaging proteins are the histones, small basic proteins around which about two turns of double-stranded DNA wrap to form a repeating unit called a nucleosome. Histones bind mainly through ionic attraction between their basic amino acids and the acidic phosphate backbone, so the interaction is largely independent of the base sequence. How tightly a region is packed, which is tuned by chemical modifications of the histones and by methylation of the DNA, helps determine which genes are accessible for transcription.

Although RNA is chemically very close to DNA, it differs in three main respects. Its sugar is ribose, which bears a hydroxyl group at the 2' position that deoxyribose lacks and which makes RNA more chemically reactive and easily broken down. RNA uses uracil in place of thymine as the base that pairs with adenine. And whereas cellular DNA is typically a double-stranded helix, RNA is usually a single and shorter strand, though it can fold back on itself and form short base-paired helices within one molecule.

Cells make several kinds of RNA, three of which are central to protein synthesis. Messenger RNA (mRNA) carries the genetic message transcribed from DNA out to the ribosome, where each codon of three nucleotides corresponds to one amino acid. Transfer RNA (tRNA) is a small molecule of roughly 80 nucleotides that carries a specific amino acid and uses a three-base anticodon to recognize the matching codon on the mRNA. Ribosomal RNA (rRNA) is the catalytic component of the ribosome, the particle that reads the mRNA and joins the amino acids together into a protein.

Beyond serving as the building blocks of nucleic acids, individual nucleotides carry out many other jobs in the cell. Nucleoside triphosphates such as adenosine triphosphate (ATP) and guanosine triphosphate (GTP) act as the cell's main carriers of chemical energy. Cyclic nucleotides such as cyclic AMP and cyclic GMP serve as intracellular signaling molecules, and nucleotides are also incorporated into key enzyme cofactors such as coenzyme A, NAD, and FAD.
