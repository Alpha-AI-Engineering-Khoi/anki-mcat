# Sensation and perception

Sensation and perception are distinct but tightly linked stages of how organisms take in the world. Sensation is the process of gathering information about the surroundings through the detection of stimuli by the sense organs, whereas perception is the identification, interpretation, and organization of that sensory information so that it can represent and be understood as the environment. The nineteenth-century physician and physicist Hermann Helmholtz captured the contrast by defining sensations as the raw elements of conscious experience that require no learning, and perceptions as the meaningful interpretations derived from the senses. A common shorthand is that sensation is a physical process while perception is a psychological one.

All perception begins with signals that travel through the nervous system after physical or chemical stimulation of the sensory system. The sequence starts with a distal stimulus, an object or event in the world, which stimulates the sense organs; those organs convert the input energy into neural activity in a step called transduction, producing the proximal stimulus, the raw pattern of neural activity. Specialized receptor cells such as mechanoreceptors, photoreceptors, chemoreceptors, and thermoreceptors carry out this conversion, changing a physical stimulus into action potentials that travel along afferent neurons toward the sensory cortices of the brain. The brain's resulting reconstruction of the original object is called the percept.

Each sense organ requires a minimum amount of stimulation before a stimulus can be detected, a quantity called the absolute threshold. It is defined as the minimum amount of stimulation necessary for a stimulus to be detected 50 percent of the time, and it is measured using signal detection methods that present stimuli of varying intensities. Human absolute thresholds can be strikingly low: classic estimates hold that, in an otherwise silent environment, a person can detect the ticking of a watch about six meters (20 feet) away, and that on a dark, clear night candlelight can be seen from roughly 48 kilometers (30 miles) away.

Beyond detecting whether a stimulus is present, the senses must detect change, and the smallest detectable difference between two stimuli is the difference threshold, also called the just-noticeable difference. Weber's law states that this just-noticeable difference is a constant fraction of the original stimulus rather than a fixed absolute amount, so larger stimuli require larger changes to be noticed. In weight discrimination, for example, if a 105-gram weight can only just be distinguished from a 100-gram one, the just-noticeable difference is 5 grams; doubling the reference to 200 grams doubles that threshold to about 10 grams, reflecting a roughly five-percent Weber fraction. This is why swapping a one-pound object for a two-pound one is easy to notice, while telling a ten-pound object from an eleven-pound one is much harder.

The quantitative study of the relationship between physical stimuli and the sensations they produce is called psychophysics, a term coined by Gustav Fechner, who published the founding work of the field in 1860. Building on Weber's law, Fechner's law proposes that the intensity of a sensation grows in proportion to the logarithm of the stimulus intensity, so that the perceived change increases far more slowly than the physical energy does. A logarithmic relationship means that when a stimulus grows by multiplication, the corresponding sensation grows only by addition; repeatedly tripling a stimulus, for instance, adds roughly equal increments to the perceived magnitude. Both laws break down at very low intensities near the absolute threshold.

Signal detection theory analyzes how observers make decisions under uncertainty, separating a genuine signal from background noise that includes both random activity within the nervous system and noise in the environment. Because the observer is an active decision-maker who sets an internal criterion, each trial falls into one of four outcomes: a hit when a present stimulus is detected, a miss when a present stimulus goes undetected, a false alarm when a stimulus is reported although absent, and a correct rejection when an absent stimulus is correctly judged absent. A central insight is that performance reflects two independent things, the observer's true sensitivity to the signal and the observer's response bias, and Green and Swets argued that traditional psychophysics could not tell these apart. Where the criterion is set depends on the costs of the errors: when a miss is far more costly than a false alarm a liberal bias is desirable, whereas repeatedly crying wolf encourages a more conservative criterion.

Sensory or neural adaptation is a gradual decline over time in the responsiveness of a sensory system to a constant, unchanging stimulus. A familiar example is resting a hand on a table: the surface is felt against the skin at first, but the sensation fades until it is virtually unnoticeable, and likewise the ticking of a clock recedes from awareness after one has spent a while in the room. All sensory systems adapt in this way, which allows neurons to keep signaling meaningful changes in the environment and effectively extends their limited response range across a wider range of stimulus intensities. Adaptation is a physiological phenomenon distinct from habituation, which is behavioral, because a person has no conscious control over it and cannot, for instance, force themselves to smell an odor to which they have already adapted.

Perception combines bottom-up and top-down processing. Bottom-up processing is data-driven, meaning that perceptions are built directly from the sensory input arriving from the environment. Top-down processing is knowledge-driven, meaning that how those sensations are interpreted is shaped by a person's available knowledge, experiences, expectations, and thoughts. An ambiguous shape shows both at work: viewed on its own it is just a pattern of lines, but placed among letters it is read as a "B" and placed among numbers it is read as "13," because context and expectation steer the interpretation. This top-down influence is captured by the idea of a perceptual set, a predisposition to perceive things in a certain way.

To turn fragmentary sensations into coherent objects, the visual system organizes elements into groups, a process described by the Gestalt principles of grouping first proposed by Gestalt psychologists. These principles include proximity, by which elements that are close together are grouped as one object; similarity, by which elements that resemble one another are grouped; closure, the tendency to see a complete figure even when part of its border is missing; good continuation, which links overlapping elements into smooth, uninterrupted forms; and common fate, which groups elements that move together in the same direction. The Gestalt view holds that the mind has an innate disposition to perceive patterns, so people readily perceive whole shapes even when the stimulus is incomplete.

Vision is often regarded as the primary human sense and offers a concrete illustration of transduction and perception. Light enters the eye and is focused onto the retina, a dense surface of photoreceptors that generate electrical nerve impulses, and humans perceive only the narrow band of electromagnetic radiation between roughly 380 and 720 nanometers as visible light. The retina holds two classes of photoreceptor: rods, which are very sensitive to light but do not distinguish colors, and cones, which distinguish colors but are less responsive in dim light, so vision in a dark room is essentially in shades of gray. Color vision arises because there are three types of cone, each most sensitive to a different range of wavelengths, and the brain extracts color by comparing their relative activation.

Hearing, or audition, is the transduction of sound waves into a neural signal by the structures of the ear. Sound is collected by the outer ear and vibrates the tympanic membrane, whose motion passes through three tiny middle-ear bones, the malleus, incus, and stapes, to the inner ear, where mechanoreceptor hair cells convert the mechanical vibration into nerve impulses. Human ears respond to vibrations across a range of about 20 to 20,000 hertz, and sensitivity to the highest frequencies declines as a person ages. From the inner ear the signal is carried along the ascending auditory pathway to the primary auditory cortex in the temporal lobe for further processing.
