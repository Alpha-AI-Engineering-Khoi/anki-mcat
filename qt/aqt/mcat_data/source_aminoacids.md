# Amino acids and proteins

Proteins are large biological macromolecules made of one or more long chains of amino acid residues, and they carry out a vast range of tasks in living organisms, including catalyzing metabolic reactions, providing structure to cells and tissues, transporting molecules, and responding to signals. A single chain of amino acids is called a polypeptide, and proteins differ from one another chiefly in the sequence of amino acids they contain. Very short chains, roughly under twenty to thirty residues, are usually called peptides rather than proteins. Amino acids are therefore the monomers, or building blocks, from which peptides and proteins are assembled.

An amino acid is an organic molecule that contains both an amino group and a carboxylic acid group. In the alpha-amino acids that build proteins, both of these groups are attached to a single central carbon called the alpha carbon, which also carries a hydrogen atom and a variable side chain known as the R group. The identity of the R group is what distinguishes one amino acid from another. Because the alpha carbon in most amino acids bears four different substituents it is a stereocenter, and the amino acids found in proteins are almost all of the L configuration; glycine is the exception, since its side chain is only a hydrogen atom, so it is the one standard amino acid that is not chiral.

In water near neutral pH, an amino acid does not exist as the neutral structure but as a zwitterion, in which the carboxyl group has lost a proton to become negatively charged and the amino group has gained a proton to become positively charged. Such an ion carries both a positive and a negative site at once, so although its net charge is zero it is misleading to call it uncharged. The high dielectric constant of water and its hydrogen-bonding network help stabilize these separated charges. In strongly acidic solution the carboxylate takes back a proton and the molecule becomes a cation, whereas in strongly basic solution the protonated amino group loses its proton and the molecule becomes an anion.

Amino acids are commonly grouped by the chemical character of their side chains. Nonpolar, hydrophobic side chains do not ionize and tend to be buried in the interior of a water-soluble protein, and interactions among them are the primary driving force that folds proteins. Polar uncharged side chains, such as those of serine, threonine, asparagine, and glutamine, readily form hydrogen bonds with water and with other residues. Acidic residues such as aspartate and glutamate carry a negative charge at neutral pH, while basic residues such as arginine, lysine, and histidine can carry a positive charge; these charged groups often sit on the protein surface to aid solubility, and oppositely charged side chains can form electrostatic contacts called salt bridges.

Whether an ionizable group is protonated depends on the pH relative to that group's pKa, the pH at which the group is half dissociated. The isoelectric point, pI, is the pH at which the amino acid carries no net charge because its positive and negative charges balance; for an amino acid with a non-ionizing side chain it is simply the average of the pKa values of the carboxyl and amino groups, whereas for one with an ionizable side chain the side-chain pKa is used instead. At its isoelectric point an amino acid has zero mobility in an electric field and shows its lowest solubility in water. Side chains can also shift their behavior with the local environment; histidine, whose imidazole group has a pKa near six, is only slightly protonated at neutral pH, which lets it act as both an acid and a base in enzyme reactions.

A peptide bond is the amide linkage that joins two amino acids, formed by a condensation, or dehydration, reaction in which the carboxyl group of one amino acid reacts with the amino group of another and a molecule of water is released. Delocalization of the nitrogen's lone pair gives this bond partial double-bond character, which makes the amide group planar and the backbone somewhat rigid. Chains of amino acids linked this way are peptides or, when long, polypeptides and proteins, with each interior residue bonded to two neighbors. A polypeptide has a free amino group at one end, the N-terminus, and a free carboxyl group at the other, the C-terminus; ribosomes build proteins starting from the N-terminus and moving toward the C-terminus, and sequences are written in that same direction by convention.

The primary structure of a protein is the linear sequence of its amino acid residues, held together by peptide bonds and conventionally numbered starting from the N-terminus. This sequence is dictated by the gene that encodes the protein and is unique to that protein. Because a water molecule is lost each time a bond forms, the units in the chain are properly called residues rather than whole amino acids. The primary sequence is decisive because it carries the information that specifies the protein's native three-dimensional shape and the folding pathway used to reach it.

Secondary structure describes regular, local folding patterns of the polypeptide backbone that are stabilized by hydrogen bonds between backbone peptide groups, specifically between an amide hydrogen and a carbonyl oxygen. The two main types, the alpha helix and the beta sheet, also called the beta pleated sheet, were proposed by Linus Pauling and coworkers in 1951. In an alpha helix the backbone coils into a spiral held by these hydrogen bonds, whereas in a beta sheet extended strands lie alongside one another and hydrogen bond between strands in either a parallel or an antiparallel arrangement. These elements form quickly and are among the first ordered structures to appear as a protein folds.

Tertiary structure is the overall three-dimensional shape of a single polypeptide chain, in which its helices and sheets pack together into a compact form. Folding is largely driven by the hydrophobic effect: nonpolar side chains cluster away from water in the protein's core, an event called hydrophobic collapse, which leaves hydrophilic side chains exposed on the surface. The shape is then stabilized by a combination of interactions, including hydrogen bonds, van der Waals forces, ionic interactions such as salt bridges, and the tight packing of side chains, and it is opposed by the loss of conformational entropy. Covalent disulfide bonds between the sulfur atoms of two cysteine residues can further lock the structure in place.

Quaternary structure arises when two or more folded polypeptide chains, called subunits, assemble into a single functional complex. It is held together by the same kinds of noncovalent interactions and disulfide bonds that stabilize tertiary structure. Complexes are named for the number of subunits, such as a dimer for two or a tetramer for four, and are called homomers if the subunits are identical or heteromers if they differ. Hemoglobin is a classic example, a heterotetramer built from two alpha chains and two beta chains.

Denaturation is the loss of a protein's folded three-dimensional structure, shifting it toward an unfolded random coil that lacks its tertiary and often its secondary structure. It can be caused by heat, extremes of pH, mechanical force, or chemical denaturants such as urea, and because structure determines function, a denatured protein usually loses its activity. A familiar example is the cooking of an egg white, which turns opaque as its proteins unfold. Some proteins can refold once normal conditions are restored, but in many cases denaturation is irreversible.
