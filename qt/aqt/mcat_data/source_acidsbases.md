# Acids and bases

In chemistry, an acid-base reaction is a chemical reaction between an acid and a base, and several complementary theories describe what qualifies as an acid or a base. These frameworks differ mainly in scope: the Arrhenius theory is the most restrictive, the Bronsted-Lowry theory is broader, and the Lewis theory has the widest definition. Under the Arrhenius definition, an acid is a substance that increases the concentration of hydrogen ions (H+, understood in water as the hydronium ion H3O+) in aqueous solution, while a base increases the concentration of hydroxide ions (OH-). A limitation of this model is that it applies only to aqueous solutions, and the reaction of an Arrhenius acid with a base, called neutralization, produces a salt and water.

The Bronsted-Lowry definition, proposed independently by Johannes Bronsted and Thomas Lowry in 1923, treats an acid as a proton (hydrogen ion) donor and a base as a proton acceptor. When an acid donates its proton it becomes its conjugate base, and when a base accepts a proton it becomes its conjugate acid, so an acid and a base always occur as a conjugate pair that differs by a single proton. Because it does not depend on forming a salt and solvent, this model is formally independent of any particular solvent and is more general than the Arrhenius picture. Water is amphoteric under this view: it can act as an acid by donating a proton to form hydroxide, or as a base by accepting a proton to form hydronium.

The Lewis definition, introduced by Gilbert N. Lewis, removes the requirement for hydrogen altogether by focusing on electron pairs. A Lewis base is a species that can donate a pair of electrons, and a Lewis acid is a species that can accept that electron pair to form a coordinate (dative) covalent bond. This is the broadest of the common theories: boron trifluoride (BF3), for instance, acts as a Lewis acid because it has an incomplete octet and can accept an electron pair from a donor such as fluoride or ammonia. The Lewis and Bronsted-Lowry views remain consistent with each other, because the combination of a hydrogen ion with hydroxide to form water is an acid-base reaction under both theories.

The pH scale specifies the acidity or basicity of an aqueous solution and is defined as the negative base-10 logarithm of the hydrogen ion activity, approximated by pH = -log10[H+]. Because the scale is logarithmic, a difference of one pH unit corresponds to a tenfold difference in hydrogen ion concentration. At 25 C a solution with pH less than 7 is acidic, one with pH greater than 7 is basic, and pH 7 is neutral; the range is commonly given as 0 to 14, though very concentrated strong acids or bases can lie outside it. The concept was introduced by Soren Sorensen in 1909, and the leading letter p is now read as "the negative decimal logarithm of".

Water undergoes self-ionization, with two water molecules in equilibrium with a hydronium ion and a hydroxide ion, described by the ion product of water Kw, which equals the product of the hydrogen ion and hydroxide ion concentrations. At 25 C, Kw is about 1.0 x 10^-14, so in pure water the hydrogen and hydroxide ion concentrations are each about 1 x 10^-7 M. The quantity pOH is the negative base-10 logarithm of the hydroxide ion concentration, and taking logarithms of the Kw expression gives pOH = pKw - pH. Since pKw is close to 14 at room temperature, this reduces to the familiar relation pH + pOH = 14 at 25 C, although the exact value shifts with temperature.

Acids and bases are classified as strong or weak according to how completely they ionize in water. A strong acid such as hydrochloric acid or sulfuric acid is essentially fully dissociated, so its hydrogen ion concentration can be taken as equal to the acid concentration, and a strong base such as sodium hydroxide is likewise treated as fully dissociated. A weak acid or weak base ionizes only partially, leaving an equilibrium mixture of the undissociated molecule and its ions, which is why finding the pH of a weak acid generally requires solving a quadratic equation rather than reading it off directly. Very strong acids are effectively leveled to the same apparent strength in water because they transfer their protons completely to form hydronium, a phenomenon called solvent leveling.

For a weak acid HA that dissociates into A- and H+, the acid dissociation constant Ka is the equilibrium constant of that reaction: Ka equals the product of the conjugate base and hydrogen ion concentrations divided by the concentration of the undissociated acid. Because Ka values span many orders of magnitude, they are usually reported on a logarithmic scale as pKa = -log10(Ka). A larger Ka, and therefore a lower pKa, corresponds to a stronger acid that is more fully dissociated at equilibrium; acetic acid, for example, has a Ka of about 1.8 x 10^-5 and a pKa of about 4.7. Any aqueous acid with a pKa below about 0 is essentially completely deprotonated and is considered strong.

For a base B, basicity is described by the base constant Kb for its protonation to the conjugate acid, with pKb = -log10(Kb). For a conjugate acid-base pair in water, Ka and Kb are linked through the ion product of water by Ka times Kb = Kw, so taking logarithms gives pKa + pKb = pKw, which is about 14 at 25 C. A practical consequence is that the stronger an acid is, the weaker its conjugate base, and the stronger a base is, the weaker its conjugate acid. Because pKb can be recovered from pKa in this way, organic chemistry often expresses the strength of a base by quoting the pKa of its conjugate acid instead of a separate pKb.

A buffer solution resists large changes in pH when a small amount of strong acid or strong base is added, or upon dilution, and is typically made from a weak acid together with its conjugate base. It works because the weak acid and its conjugate base are held in equilibrium: added hydrogen ions are consumed by the conjugate base and added hydroxide is consumed by the weak acid, so by Le Chatelier's principle the free hydrogen ion concentration changes far less than it otherwise would. Buffering is essential in biological systems, where enzymes require a nearly constant pH; the carbonic acid and bicarbonate system, for instance, helps hold human blood between about pH 7.35 and 7.45. A buffer's ability to absorb added acid or base, called its buffer capacity, is greatest when the pH equals the pKa of the weak acid.

Rearranging the Ka expression yields the Henderson-Hasselbalch equation, pH = pKa + log10([A-]/[HA]), which relates the pH of a buffer to the pKa and to the ratio of conjugate base to weak acid. When the acid is exactly half neutralized the concentrations of the acid and its conjugate base are equal, the logarithm term becomes zero, and the pH equals the pKa. A useful buffer therefore operates near its pKa, with effective buffering roughly over the range pKa plus or minus 1, so a buffer is chosen to have a pKa close to the desired pH. Beyond about pKa plus or minus 2 the substance is essentially all acid or all conjugate base and little buffering capacity remains.

Titration is a quantitative method that determines the unknown concentration of an analyte by adding measured volumes of a titrant of known concentration until the reaction is complete. In an acid-base titration the equivalence point is the theoretical point at which the moles of added titrant exactly match the moles of analyte, whereas the endpoint is what is actually observed, usually a color change of an added indicator; the small mismatch between them is the indicator error. A titration curve plots pH against the volume of titrant added and has a sigmoid shape that rises steeply near the equivalence point. For a strong acid and strong base the jump at the equivalence point is large so many indicators work, but for a weak acid titrated with a strong base the equivalence point is basic (roughly pH 8 to 10), so an indicator such as phenolphthalein, whose color change brackets that pH, is chosen to reduce error.

A polyprotic acid can donate more than one proton, and each successive loss has its own stepwise dissociation constant, written Ka1, Ka2, and so on. The successive pKa values increase, because pulling a positively charged proton away from an already negatively charged species takes additional energy; phosphoric acid (H3PO4), for example, has pKa values of roughly 2.1, 7.2, and 12.4 for its three protons. When successive pKa values are well separated, each proton is removed in a distinct stage, so the titration curve of a polyprotic acid can display more than one equivalence point.
