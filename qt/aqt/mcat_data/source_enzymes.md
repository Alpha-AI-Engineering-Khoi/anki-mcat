# Enzymes: structure, catalysis, and kinetics

Enzymes are biological catalysts, usually proteins, that accelerate the chemical reactions of metabolism without being consumed and without being permanently changed themselves. The molecules an enzyme acts on are called its substrates, which it converts into products, and nearly every metabolic process in a cell depends on enzyme catalysis to run at a useful rate. Enzymes are also highly specific, and a small number of biological catalysts are not proteins at all but catalytic RNA molecules called ribozymes.

An enzyme speeds a reaction by lowering its activation energy, the energy barrier that must be crossed to reach the transition state, which is the highest-energy arrangement along the reaction path; enzymes typically achieve this by binding and stabilizing that transition state. Like any catalyst, an enzyme does not shift the position of the reaction's equilibrium and cannot by itself turn an unfavorable reaction into a favorable one: it drives the same reaction in the same direction it would go without the enzyme, only much faster. The overall free-energy change of the reaction (for example ΔG < 0 for a spontaneous process) is left unchanged, so the enzyme only hastens the approach to equilibrium; cells can, however, couple a favorable reaction such as ATP hydrolysis to an unfavorable one to push it forward.

Binding and catalysis take place at the active site, which is formed by a binding site that orients the substrate and a catalytic site (often just a few amino acid residues) that carries out the chemistry, while the rest of the protein holds this pocket in the right shape. The lock-and-key model, proposed by Emil Fischer in 1894, pictured the active site and substrate as rigid, geometrically complementary shapes that fit exactly; it explains enzyme specificity but not how enzymes stabilize the transition state. Daniel Koshland's 1958 induced-fit model refined this idea: because enzymes are flexible, the active site is reshaped as the substrate binds, molding the catalytic groups into the positions needed for the reaction.

Many enzymes require an additional non-protein component called a cofactor in order to be active. Cofactors come in two broad kinds: inorganic ions such as zinc or magnesium (for instance the zinc ion built into carbonic anhydrase), and organic molecules called coenzymes, which are often derived from vitamins and include group carriers such as NAD+ and coenzyme A. An enzyme that is missing its required cofactor is an inactive apoenzyme, whereas the complete, catalytically active enzyme together with its cofactor is a holoenzyme. Coenzymes that are only loosely bound behave like cosubstrates, ferrying chemical groups or electrons between different enzymes, while a cofactor that is tightly or even covalently bound and stays attached through the catalytic cycle is called a prosthetic group.

Because enzymes are proteins, their activity is sensitive to conditions such as pH and temperature, and each enzyme has an optimum at which it works fastest. Activity generally rises with temperature until the protein begins to unfold, so the reaction rate peaks at an intermediate temperature and then drops off. Moving far from the optimal pH or temperature, or exposure to chemical denaturants, causes denaturation, the loss of the enzyme's three-dimensional structure and therefore of its function; optimal pH also varies widely, from about 1.5 for the stomach protease pepsin to roughly 8 for trypsin.

Michaelis–Menten kinetics is the simplest description of how the initial rate of a single-substrate enzyme reaction depends on substrate concentration. As substrate concentration increases, the rate rises steeply and then levels off, approaching a maximum rate Vmax at which the enzyme is saturated and essentially all active sites are occupied by substrate. The Michaelis constant Km is the substrate concentration that produces half of Vmax; a small Km reflects a high affinity of the enzyme for its substrate, and Km depends on the identities of the enzyme and substrate and on conditions, but not on how much enzyme is present. Well below Km the rate is approximately first-order, increasing nearly in proportion to substrate concentration, while well above Km it becomes zero-order and almost independent of substrate.

The turnover number kcat, also called the catalytic constant, is the maximum number of substrate molecules a single active site converts to product per unit time when the enzyme is saturated, and it is related to the maximum rate by Vmax equal to kcat times the total enzyme concentration. The ratio kcat/Km, known as the catalytic efficiency or specificity constant, gauges how well an enzyme works at low substrate levels and is the most useful single number for comparing different enzymes or comparing one enzyme's performance on different substrates. This ratio cannot exceed the rate at which enzyme and substrate diffuse together, on the order of a hundred million to a billion per molar per second; enzymes that reach this diffusion limit, such as carbonic anhydrase and fumarase, are described as catalytically or kinetically perfect.

Enzyme inhibitors are molecules that reduce or abolish enzyme activity, and reversible inhibitors are traditionally classified by how they affect Km and Vmax as substrate concentration is varied. A competitive inhibitor binds the active site so that substrate and inhibitor cannot occupy the enzyme at the same time, and such inhibitors often resemble the substrate in structure; the anticancer drug methotrexate, which mimics folate to block dihydrofolate reductase, is a classic example. Because adding enough substrate can out-compete the inhibitor, competitive inhibition raises the apparent Km while leaving Vmax unchanged.

A noncompetitive inhibitor binds at a site other than the active site and does so with equal affinity to the free enzyme and to the enzyme-substrate complex; it does not interfere with substrate binding, so Km stays the same, but it lowers Vmax and cannot be overcome by adding more substrate. An uncompetitive inhibitor, by contrast, binds only to the enzyme-substrate complex and not to free enzyme, which lowers both Vmax and Km; this type is comparatively rare, and because it needs the complex to have formed it is most effective at high substrate concentrations.

A mixed inhibitor can bind either the free enzyme or the enzyme-substrate complex, usually with different affinities for the two forms, so it blends the behavior of competitive and noncompetitive inhibition and generally alters both Km and Vmax. Mixed inhibition typically results from binding at an allosteric site that changes the enzyme's conformation and reduces the substrate's affinity for the active site; raising the substrate concentration can lessen this kind of inhibition but cannot fully relieve it.

Allosteric regulation acts through effector molecules that bind a regulatory (allosteric) site separate from the active site, producing a conformational change that either activates or inhibits the enzyme, with activators increasing activity and inhibitors decreasing it. Many allosteric enzymes are built from several subunits and show cooperativity, in which substrate binding at one site changes the affinity of the other sites, giving an S-shaped (sigmoidal) rate curve rather than the usual hyperbolic one. The concerted (Monod–Wyman–Changeux) and sequential (Koshland–Némethy–Filmer) models both explain this by having subunits shift between a tense, low-affinity state and a relaxed, high-affinity state.

A widespread use of inhibition in cells is feedback inhibition, a form of negative feedback in which the end product of a metabolic pathway inhibits an enzyme acting early in that same pathway, often at the first committed and irreversible step, so the cell slows production once enough product has accumulated. Glycolysis is a well-studied example: when ATP is plentiful it binds an allosteric site on phosphofructokinase and inhibits it, slowing the pathway and helping keep the cell's ATP level steady. This kind of regulation prevents wasteful overproduction of intermediates and helps maintain a stable internal environment.
