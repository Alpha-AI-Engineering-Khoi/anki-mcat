# Learning and conditioning

Learning is the process of acquiring new understanding, knowledge, behaviors, skills, values, attitudes, and preferences, and the capacity for it is found in humans, other animals, and even some machines. Some learning happens immediately from a single event, such as being burned by a hot stove, while much knowledge and skill accumulate gradually from repeated experience, and the changes learning produces can last a lifetime. Psychologists distinguish non-associative learning, such as habituation, in which an innate response weakens as a harmless stimulus is repeated, from associative learning, in which an organism learns a relationship between two stimuli or between a behavior and its consequences.

Classical conditioning, also called Pavlovian or respondent conditioning, is a form of associative learning in which a biologically potent stimulus is paired with a previously neutral one until the neutral stimulus by itself comes to trigger a response. The unconditioned stimulus (US) is one that produces a reaction without any prior learning, and the reflexive reaction it evokes is the unconditioned response (UR). After a neutral stimulus is repeatedly paired with the US, it becomes a conditioned stimulus (CS) that on its own elicits a learned reaction called the conditioned response (CR); unlike the UR, the CR is acquired through experience and tends to be less permanent.

The classic demonstration comes from the Russian physiologist Ivan Pavlov, who studied digestion in dogs and noticed that they salivated not only when given food but also at cues that regularly preceded it. In his experiments the food was the unconditioned stimulus and the salivation it caused was the unconditioned response, while a neutral signal such as the sound of a metronome or a bell initially produced no salivation. After the signal was presented just before the food over many trials, the dogs began to salivate to the signal alone, so the sound had become a conditioned stimulus and the salivation to it a conditioned response.

During acquisition the conditioned stimulus and unconditioned stimulus are paired repeatedly, and the conditioned response usually grows gradually in strength across trials, although a single pairing can sometimes be enough. If the conditioned stimulus is later presented many times without the unconditioned stimulus, the conditioned response weakens and eventually stops, a process called extinction. Extinction does not erase the original learning, however, as shown by spontaneous recovery: after a rest period the extinguished conditioned response can reappear when the conditioned stimulus is presented again, usually in a weaker form than before.

Two complementary phenomena describe how narrowly conditioning is tied to a particular cue. Stimulus generalization occurs when a stimulus similar to the conditioned stimulus also elicits the conditioned response, and typically the more closely the new stimulus resembles the original, the stronger the response it evokes. Stimulus discrimination is the opposite tendency, in which an organism learns to respond to one stimulus but not to another; this can be produced by pairing one stimulus with the unconditioned stimulus while presenting the second stimulus without it.

Operant conditioning, also called instrumental conditioning, is a learning process in which voluntary behaviors are strengthened or weakened by their consequences, in contrast to the reflexive responses shaped by classical conditioning. Edward Thorndike laid its groundwork with his law of effect, drawn from watching cats escape from puzzle boxes: behaviors followed by satisfying consequences tend to be repeated, while those followed by unpleasant consequences become less likely. B. F. Skinner, often called the father of operant conditioning, extended this work using the operant conditioning chamber, or Skinner box, and defined reinforcement as any consequence that makes a behavior more frequent and punishment as any consequence that makes it less frequent.

In this terminology the words positive and negative refer to whether a stimulus is added or removed, not to whether it is pleasant or unpleasant. Positive reinforcement strengthens a behavior by adding a desirable stimulus after it, as when a rat presses a lever, receives food, and then presses the lever more often. Negative reinforcement strengthens a behavior by removing or preventing an aversive stimulus, as when taking aspirin relieves a headache or putting on headphones ends a loud noise; because the unpleasant state goes away, the behavior that ended it becomes more likely to recur.

Punishment works in the opposite direction from reinforcement, making a behavior less likely, and it too can be positive or negative. Positive punishment adds an aversive stimulus after a behavior, as when touching a hot stove burns a person's hand so that they no longer touch the stove. Negative punishment removes a desirable stimulus, as when a privilege such as screen time is taken away or a child is placed in time out and loses the chance to play; in each case the behavior that led to the loss becomes less frequent in the future.

Reinforcers are also classified by how they acquire their power to strengthen behavior. A primary reinforcer, also called an unconditioned reinforcer, does not depend on prior learning and is typically tied to survival, with food, water, and sex as common examples. A secondary or conditioned reinforcer, such as money, gains its reinforcing value only after being paired with an existing reinforcer; a helpful rule of thumb is that a primary reinforcer is something a caveman would find inherently desirable, whereas a secondary reinforcer like a dollar bill would mean nothing without learning.

Behavior need not be reinforced every time it occurs, and the schedule of reinforcement strongly shapes how a response is performed and how long it persists once reinforcement stops. Ratio schedules deliver reinforcement based on the number of responses made, while interval schedules deliver it based on elapsed time, and each type can be fixed, using a constant requirement, or variable, changing around an average. Fixed schedules often produce a pause right after reinforcement, a fixed interval schedule yields a scalloped pattern in which responding accelerates as the reward time approaches, and a variable ratio schedule, such as a slot machine, generates very high and persistent responding. Because they are less predictable, intermittently reinforced behaviors resist extinction more than continuously reinforced ones, with ratio schedules generally more resistant than interval schedules and variable schedules more resistant than fixed ones.

Not all learning requires directly experiencing consequences; observational learning, a form of social learning, occurs by watching the behavior of others and its outcomes. Albert Bandura demonstrated this with the Bobo doll experiment, in which children who watched an adult behave aggressively toward an inflatable doll were more likely to imitate that aggression than children who had seen a calm model. Bandura's social cognitive theory holds that observational learning depends on four processes: attention to the model, retention of what was observed, the motor ability to reproduce the action, and motivation to perform it. He also distinguished learning from performance, noting that an observer may acquire a behavior yet carry it out only when motivated, for example by vicarious reinforcement that is seen when the model is rewarded.
