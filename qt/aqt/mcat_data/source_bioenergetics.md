# Bioenergetics and cellular respiration

Adenosine triphosphate, or ATP, is the molecule cells use as their main carrier of usable energy, and it is often called the molecular unit of currency for energy transfer inside the cell. It is a nucleoside triphosphate built from the base adenine, the sugar ribose, and a chain of three phosphate groups. When the bond to the terminal phosphate is broken by hydrolysis, ATP is converted to adenosine diphosphate (ADP) and inorganic phosphate, releasing about 30.5 kJ/mol of free energy under standard conditions, and this ATP-to-ADP conversion is the principal way cells supply energy for work such as biosynthesis, muscle contraction, and moving molecules across membranes. Cells continually rebuild ATP from ADP through processes such as substrate-level phosphorylation and oxidative phosphorylation.

Cellular respiration is a set of catabolic, largely redox reactions in which cells oxidize fuels such as sugars, using an inorganic electron acceptor, to transfer the chemical energy of those nutrients into ATP. When the electron acceptor is molecular oxygen the process is aerobic respiration, and the complete aerobic oxidation of glucose can be summarized as glucose plus six O2 yielding six CO2, six H2O, and energy. This overall reaction is strongly exergonic, releasing a large amount of free energy with a standard free-energy change of roughly -2880 kJ per mole of glucose, so it proceeds spontaneously.

In eukaryotes the reactions are organized into stages in different cellular compartments. Glycolysis takes place in the cytoplasm, while the reactions after glycolysis, namely pyruvate oxidation, the citric acid cycle, and oxidative phosphorylation, occur in the mitochondria; in prokaryotes, which lack mitochondria, these later steps happen in the cytoplasm and at the plasma membrane. The three main ATP-producing pathways of this scheme are glycolysis, the citric acid cycle coupled to oxidative phosphorylation, and, for fats, beta-oxidation.

Glycolysis is a sequence of about ten enzyme-catalyzed reactions, carried out in the cytosol, that splits one molecule of glucose into two molecules of pyruvate, and it proceeds whether or not oxygen is present. Along the way it captures free energy as ATP and NADH: an early preparatory phase invests two ATP to phosphorylate and destabilize the sugar, and a later pay-off phase produces four ATP by substrate-level phosphorylation together with two NADH. The result is a net gain of two ATP and two NADH per glucose, plus the two pyruvate molecules that feed the following stages.

When oxygen is available, the pyruvate produced by glycolysis is transported into the mitochondrion and oxidized by the pyruvate dehydrogenase complex, which converts it into an acetyl group carried by coenzyme A, known as acetyl-CoA. Each pyruvate that undergoes this oxidative decarboxylation releases one molecule of CO2 and reduces one NAD+ to NADH. Because one glucose yields two pyruvate, this linking step produces two acetyl-CoA, two NADH, and two CO2 per glucose before the citric acid cycle begins.

The citric acid cycle, also called the Krebs cycle or tricarboxylic acid (TCA) cycle, runs in the mitochondrial matrix and completes the oxidation of the acetyl group. Each turn begins when the two-carbon acetyl group from acetyl-CoA is joined to the four-carbon oxaloacetate to form six-carbon citrate, which is then rearranged and oxidized through a series of intermediates that release two molecules of CO2 and regenerate oxaloacetate. One turn captures the released energy as three NADH, one FADH2, and one GTP that is readily converted to ATP; since two acetyl-CoA are made per glucose, the cycle turns twice, giving six NADH, two FADH2, and two GTP per glucose.

NAD+ and FAD are electron carriers that shuttle the high-energy electrons harvested during these oxidations. As fuels are oxidized in glycolysis, pyruvate oxidation, and the citric acid cycle, NAD+ is reduced to NADH and FAD is reduced to FADH2, storing electrons that have a high transfer potential. These reduced carriers deliver their electrons to the electron transport chain, where they are reoxidized back to NAD+ and FAD so they can be used again; this recycling is why the citric acid cycle is obligately aerobic even though the cycle itself does not use oxygen.

Oxidative phosphorylation consists of the electron transport chain and chemiosmosis working together. Electrons from NADH and FADH2 pass down a series of protein complexes embedded in the inner mitochondrial membrane through a chain of redox reactions, and the energy released is used to pump protons from the matrix into the intermembrane space, creating an electrochemical gradient called the proton-motive force. Oxygen serves as the final electron acceptor at the end of the chain and is reduced to water. The stored energy of the gradient is tapped when protons flow back across the membrane through the enzyme ATP synthase, a rotary motor that uses the proton flow to phosphorylate ADP into ATP.

Cells make ATP in two distinct ways. In substrate-level phosphorylation, an enzyme transfers a phosphate group directly to ADP from a high-energy substrate, which is how the small ATP yields of glycolysis and one step of the citric acid cycle are produced, and it does not require oxygen. In oxidative phosphorylation, by contrast, ATP synthase makes ATP using the proton gradient generated by the electron transport chain, and this accounts for most of the ATP produced during aerobic respiration.

Respiration is classified by its terminal electron acceptor: aerobic respiration uses oxygen, whereas anaerobic respiration uses a different inorganic acceptor such as nitrate or sulfate and is carried out by certain bacteria and archaea. Fermentation is a separate anaerobic route that is not respiration, because it uses no external electron acceptor; instead it reoxidizes NADH back to NAD+ in the cytoplasm so that glycolysis can continue when oxygen is scarce. The identity of the waste product depends on the organism, as skeletal muscle carries out lactic acid fermentation while yeast performs alcoholic fermentation, producing ethanol and carbon dioxide.

The complete aerobic oxidation of one glucose molecule captures far more energy than anaerobic metabolism does. Biology textbooks have traditionally cited a figure of about 36 to 38 ATP per glucose, but this theoretical maximum is not actually reached, and current estimates place the real yield closer to roughly 30 to 32 ATP because protons leak across the inner membrane and because moving pyruvate, phosphate, and ADP into the mitochondrion carries an energy cost. Fermentation is far less efficient, yielding only the two ATP that come from glycolysis, although it produces that ATP quickly.
