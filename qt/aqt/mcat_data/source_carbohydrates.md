# Carbohydrates

A carbohydrate is a sugar, also called a saccharide, or a molecule derived from one, and carbohydrates form one of the major families of biomolecules alongside amino acids, fats, and nucleic acids. For the simplest carbohydrates the ratio of carbon to hydrogen to oxygen is 1:2:1, so they are often written with the empirical formula (CH2O)n. Chemically, saccharides are polyhydroxy aldehydes or polyhydroxy ketones, together with polymers built from such units through linkages of the acetal type.

Carbohydrates carry out many roles in living organisms. Polysaccharides act as energy stores, such as starch in plants and glycogen in animals, and as structural materials, such as cellulose in plant cell walls and chitin in the exoskeletons of arthropods and the walls of fungi. The five-carbon sugar ribose is an important component of coenzymes such as ATP, FAD, and NAD and forms the backbone of RNA, while the related deoxyribose is a component of DNA. Sugars and their derivatives also participate in the immune system, fertilization, the prevention of pathogenesis, blood clotting, and development.

Monosaccharides, or simple sugars, are the simplest carbohydrates because they cannot be hydrolyzed into smaller carbohydrates, and each one is a polyhydroxy aldehyde or a polyhydroxy ketone. They can be classified in two complementary ways. By the position of the carbonyl group, a sugar that carries a terminal aldehyde is an aldose, whereas one whose carbonyl is an internal ketone, usually at carbon 2, is a ketose. By the number of carbon atoms a monosaccharide is called a triose, tetrose, pentose, hexose, or heptose for three through seven carbons, and the two schemes combine into names such as aldohexose and ketotriose; glucose is an aldohexose, fructose is a ketohexose, and ribose and deoxyribose are pentoses.

In an open-chain monosaccharide every carbon that carries a hydroxyl group is a chiral center except the terminal carbons and, in a ketose, the carbonyl carbon, so most sugars exist as several stereoisomers. The D and L system assigns a configuration by looking at the chiral carbon farthest from the carbonyl group and relating it to glyceraldehyde, the three-carbon reference sugar to which all such centers are conventionally compared. In a Fischer projection the sugar is designated D if the hydroxyl on that reference carbon points to the right and L if it points to the left. These prefixes describe spatial configuration only and do not by themselves indicate the direction in which the compound rotates plane-polarized light.

Monosaccharides with more than four carbons do not remain open-chained in solution; instead one of the molecule's own hydroxyl groups adds to the carbonyl carbon in an intramolecular reaction that closes a ring bridged by a single oxygen atom. This ring bears a hemiacetal group if the sugar was an aldose or a hemiketal group if it was a ketose, and the reaction is readily reversible so the open and closed forms interconvert. Six-membered rings are called pyranoses and five-membered rings are called furanoses, by analogy with the compounds pyran and furan. For many sugars, including glucose, the cyclic forms strongly predominate over the open-chain form in solution.

Ring closure turns the former carbonyl carbon into a new stereocenter called the anomeric carbon, so each cyclic sugar can form in two configurations known as anomers and labeled alpha and beta. The two are distinguished by the relationship between the configuration at the anomeric carbon and the reference stereocenter farthest from it in the ring. Because the ring can open and reclose, the anomers interconvert in solution through the open-chain form in a process called mutarotation; a solution of D-glucose, for example, settles toward roughly 64 percent of the beta anomer and 36 percent of the alpha anomer no matter which pure form was first dissolved. Anomers are diastereomers, so they can differ in physical properties such as melting point and specific rotation.

Two drawing conventions are used for sugars. A Fischer projection depicts the open-chain form as a vertical backbone and fixes the handedness of every chiral carbon by placing each hydroxyl to the left or right. A Haworth projection depicts the cyclic form as a flat ring seen edge-on with substituents drawn above or below the plane; for a D-aldohexose in the pyranose form the alpha anomer has the anomeric hydroxyl pointing below the ring and the beta anomer has it above. A convenient rule connects the two pictures: a group written on the right in a Fischer projection points downward in the Haworth projection, and a group on the left points upward.

A glycosidic bond is an ether-type linkage that joins the anomeric carbon of a sugar, through its hemiacetal or hemiketal group, to a hydroxyl or other group on a second molecule, forming a compound called a glycoside. When the two units are monosaccharides the reaction is a condensation that converts a hemiacetal into a full acetal and releases a molecule of water. Because the linking atom is usually the oxygen that remains, these are O-glycosidic bonds, but the oxygen can be replaced by nitrogen, sulfur, or carbon to give N-, S-, or C-linkages, as in the N-glycosidic bond that attaches a nucleobase to the sugar of a nucleoside. When the anomeric carbon takes part in the bond, the linkage is further described as alpha or beta according to the configuration at that carbon.

Disaccharides are two monosaccharides joined by a glycosidic bond. In maltose, two glucose units are linked by an alpha-1,4 bond. In lactose, the sugar of milk, galactose is joined to glucose by a beta-1,4 bond. In sucrose, common table sugar from cane and beet, glucose and fructose are linked through the anomeric carbons of both sugars in an alpha-1,beta-2 bond. Because that sucrose linkage ties up both anomeric carbons, neither ring can open, which sets sucrose chemically apart from maltose and lactose.

Polysaccharides are polymers of a large number of monosaccharides joined by glycosidic bonds, and they function mainly in storage or structure. Starch, the storage polymer of plants, is a mixture of amylose, a largely linear chain of glucose joined by alpha-1,4 bonds, and amylopectin, which also carries alpha-1,6 branch points; glycogen, the animal storage polymer, resembles amylopectin but is more highly branched, a feature that lets it be mobilized quickly. Cellulose, a structural polymer of plant cell walls and the most abundant organic molecule in nature, is instead glucose joined by beta linkages. The difference in linkage is decisive for function, because humans and most animals have enzymes that hydrolyze the alpha bonds of starch and glycogen but lack the enzyme needed to break the beta bonds of cellulose, so cellulose passes through undigested.

A reducing sugar can act as a reducing agent because it keeps at least one free anomeric carbon in the form of a hemiacetal or hemiketal. In basic aqueous solution that cyclic hemiacetal is in equilibrium with a small amount of the open-chain aldehyde, which mild oxidizing reagents such as Benedict's and Fehling's solutions oxidize while being reduced themselves, converting the blue copper(II) reagent into a brick-red precipitate of copper(I) oxide. Monosaccharides such as glucose and disaccharides such as maltose and lactose are reducing because each retains a free anomeric carbon that can open. Sucrose is non-reducing because its glycosidic bond locks the anomeric carbons of both glucose and fructose as a full acetal, so no ring can open to an aldehyde.

Sugars illustrate several related kinds of stereoisomerism. Enantiomers are stereoisomers that are mirror images of each other, whereas diastereomers are stereoisomers that are not mirror images. Epimers are diastereomers that differ in configuration at just one stereocenter, and anomers are the special case of epimers that differ at the anomeric carbon formed during ring closure. Because each additional chiral center doubles the number of possibilities, a monosaccharide with n chiral carbons can have as many as two raised to the power n stereoisomers, so a single aldohexose framework yields sixteen distinct stereoisomers.
