# Kinematics and translational motion

Kinematics is the branch of physics that describes the geometry of motion, meaning how an object's position, velocity, and acceleration change over time, without considering the forces that set the object moving. For this reason it is often called the geometry of motion, and it is distinguished from dynamics, also known as kinetics, which studies how forces affect bodies. Every description of motion is made with respect to a chosen frame of reference, and a position is given by a vector drawn from the origin of that frame to the object, expressing both how far the object is from the origin and in what direction. Because motion is always relative to a reference frame, the same object can be assigned different positions and velocities in different frames.

Motion involves two related but distinct measures of how far an object has gone. Distance travelled is a scalar, the total length of the path covered, described by a magnitude alone. Displacement is a vector, the change in position from start to finish, described by both a magnitude and a direction. Because a path can curve or double back while the straight-line change in position does not, the distance travelled is always greater than or equal to the magnitude of the displacement.

Speed and velocity form a similar scalar and vector pair. Speed is the scalar magnitude of the velocity and tells only how fast an object is moving; in SI units it is measured in metres per second. Velocity is a vector that gives both the speed and the direction of motion, so "5 metres per second" states a speed while "5 metres per second east" states a velocity. To have a constant velocity an object must keep both a constant speed and a constant direction, which means moving in a straight line, so a car rounding a curve at a steady 20 kilometres per hour has constant speed but a changing velocity and is therefore accelerating. Over any interval the average speed is greater than or equal to the magnitude of the average velocity, because distance never decreases while displacement can decrease or change direction.

Velocity can be reported either as an average over an interval or as an instantaneous value at a single moment. Average velocity is the change in position divided by the elapsed time, written v_avg = Δx / Δt. Instantaneous velocity is the limit of the average velocity as the time interval shrinks toward zero, that is, the derivative of position with respect to time, and at every instant it points tangent to the object's path. Intuitively, the instantaneous velocity is the velocity the object would keep moving at if it stopped accelerating at that moment.

Acceleration is the rate of change of velocity, and like velocity it is a vector with both a magnitude and a direction; its SI unit is metres per second squared. Because velocity carries direction as well as magnitude, an object accelerates whenever its speed changes, its direction changes, or both change at once, so an object moving at constant speed around a circle is still accelerating. Average acceleration is the change in velocity divided by the time interval, a_avg = Δv / Δt, while instantaneous acceleration is the derivative of velocity with respect to time, equivalently the second derivative of position with respect to time. When motion is confined to a straight line, these vector quantities can be treated as signed scalars.

When the acceleration is constant, a set of kinematic equations, often called the SUVAT equations, links displacement, initial velocity, final velocity, acceleration, and time. Three of the most useful are v = v0 + a t, x = x0 + v0 t + 1/2 a t^2, and v^2 = v0^2 + 2 a Δx, where v0 is the initial velocity and Δx is the displacement. A fourth relation, x - x0 = 1/2 (v0 + v) t, follows from the fact that under constant acceleration the average velocity equals half the sum of the initial and final velocities. Each equation contains four of the five quantities, so knowing any three of them is enough to solve for the remaining two.

A central example of constant acceleration is free fall, the motion of a body under gravity when resistance to motion is negligible. In that case the acceleration does not depend on the object's mass but only on the gravitational field strength g, also called the acceleration due to gravity, which near Earth's surface is about 9.8 metres per second squared and is directed downward. For a ball thrown straight up, if upward is taken as positive then the acceleration is negative g throughout the flight because gravity always acts downward, and the ball is momentarily at rest, with velocity zero, at the highest point of its path before it falls back.

Projectile motion is the motion of an object launched into the air that then moves under gravity alone, with air resistance neglected; in this idealized model the path is a parabola, a result first shown by Galileo. The motion separates into independent horizontal and vertical parts that do not affect each other. Because gravity acts only vertically, the horizontal velocity stays constant while the vertical motion is simply free fall with a downward acceleration equal to g. This independence is why an object dropped from rest and one launched horizontally from the same height reach the ground at the same time, since the time to fall depends only on the height and g and not on any horizontal velocity.

Graphs of motion are read through their slopes and areas. On a position versus time graph, the slope of the tangent line at a point is the instantaneous velocity, and the slope of the secant line between two points is the average velocity over that interval. On a velocity versus time graph, the slope of the curve gives the acceleration, and the area under the curve gives the displacement. In the same way, on an acceleration versus time graph the area under the curve gives the change in velocity.

Relative velocity describes how fast and in what direction one object moves as measured from another, and it is found by subtracting velocity vectors, so the velocity of object A relative to object B is v_A minus v_B. Along a single straight line this reduces to combining speeds, adding them when the two objects move in opposite directions and subtracting when they move in the same direction. In Newtonian mechanics the relative velocity of two objects does not depend on which inertial reference frame is chosen to measure it.
